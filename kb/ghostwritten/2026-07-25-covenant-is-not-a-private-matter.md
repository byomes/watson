When God commanded Joshua to build a memorial at Gilgal from twelve stones pulled out of the Jordan riverbed, He specified something that could be easy to overlook. He did not say choose twelve strong men and send them for stones. He said choose twelve men, one from each tribe. The distinction matters enormously, because the instruction is not primarily about logistics. It is about theology. Every tribe of Israel had to be represented in the building of that memorial because the covenant the memorial was meant to preserve was not the property of any single tribe. It belonged to all of them together. One stone, one tribe, one collective act of remembrance.

This kind of communal covenant logic runs against the grain of how most of us in modern Western Christianity tend to think about faith. We have been shaped by a profoundly individualistic culture, and we bring those assumptions into our reading of Scripture without always realizing it. When we hear the word covenant, we think of a personal agreement. When we hear about salvation, we think of a private transaction. When we sing about what God has done, we tend to think first about what He has done for me. And all of that is true as far as it goes. God does meet us personally. He does save us individually. He does act on behalf of each of His people as though each one is the only one. But if we stop there, we have only told half the story, and the half we left out is the half that Joshua 4 is most insistent upon.

The covenant God made with Abraham was not a covenant with a private citizen. It was a covenant with the father of a nation. When God said to Abraham in Genesis 12 that He would bless those who blessed him and give him a particular piece of land as a forever inheritance, He was not arranging something between two individuals who happened to find each other agreeable. He was establishing the founding document of a people. Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, the twelve tribes, the wilderness generation, the Joshua generation, and every generation that would come after them were all living inside the same covenant story. The stones at Gilgal were built to make that visible. You are not here because of what you personally did. You are here because of who your God is and what He has been doing since long before you were born.

The twelve men who walked into the riverbed to retrieve those stones had to pass through the space around the Ark of the Covenant, that gap God had commanded to be maintained between His visible presence and the people. They walked into holy ground to do a communal task. And then each of them, with a stone on his shoulder, walked miles to Gilgal to add it to a pile that would stand as a single unified monument. Not twelve separate memorials, one per tribe. One memorial, built from twelve stones, belonging to everyone. The unity of the monument declared the unity of the covenant.

We need to recover this way of seeing ourselves. When we are saved, we are not saved into isolation. We are adopted into a family, grafted into a people, brought into a kingdom that has been under construction since the garden. The New Testament language for this is equally corporate. We are the body of Christ, each member belonging to the others. We are a royal priesthood, a holy nation, a people for God's own possession. These are not metaphors for individuals who happen to share a building on Sunday mornings. They describe a genuine community of covenant, a people whose story is held in common, whose inheritance is shared, whose God has been faithful across generations in ways that no one person can contain.

This matters practically because it changes how we relate to one another inside the church. If my faith is entirely personal, then your struggle is ultimately your problem and your victory is ultimately your celebration. But if we are covenant people together, then what God does in your life is part of a story I have a stake in. The stone you carry to the pile belongs to the same memorial as mine. Your tribe's stone and my tribe's stone are building the same monument. We are not parallel private arrangements with the same God. We are one people under one covenant, called to remember together what He has done and to carry that memory forward together into the next generation.

It also changes how we think about the inheritance we carry. Too often we treat our personal testimony as primarily a personal possession. What God has done in my life is my story to keep or share as I see fit. But the Gilgal logic will not let us rest there. The stones were not carried to twelve separate homes. They were stacked together in one place, in public, where everyone could see them, where the children of every tribe could stand before them and ask the question the memorial was designed to prompt. What God has done in our lives is not just ours. It is part of the covenant record that the whole community of faith is responsible to maintain and hand down.

The church has always been one generation away from forgetting this. In every era, the temptation is to reduce the faith to a collection of private experiences that happen to occur in the same building at the same time. Joshua 4 will not allow it. God insisted on twelve stones because twelve tribes needed to see themselves in the monument. He insisted on one memorial because there is one covenant people, one story, one God who has been faithful across every generation of it. We are not here by accident or by individual spiritual achievement. We are here because we have been brought into something that began long before us and will continue long after us, and we are responsible to carry our stone to the pile.
