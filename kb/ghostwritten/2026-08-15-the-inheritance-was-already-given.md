We tend to read stories of conquest as stories of effort. Someone plans, someone fights, someone wins, and the prize belongs to whoever proved strongest. That framework feels natural to us because it matches almost every other story we know. But it is the wrong lens for Joshua chapter six, and if we bring it to the text we will miss the single most important word in the entire chapter.

The word is a tense. When the Lord speaks to Joshua before the walls of Jericho, He does not say, "I am delivering this city to you," and He does not say, "I will deliver this city to you." He says, "I have delivered Jericho into your hands." Past tense. Completed action. From God's vantage point, the outcome was already settled before the first Israelite foot ever moved. What Israel was about to do was not win a city. It was receive one.

This did not begin at Jericho. It began four hundred and seventy years earlier, when God spoke to Abraham and promised him land, offspring, and blessing as a covenant gift, not a covenant wage. For nearly five centuries that promise sat unclaimed, waiting through slavery in Egypt, waiting through wilderness wandering, waiting through the deaths of an entire generation who never saw it fulfilled. And when the moment finally came, God did not ask Israel to earn what He had already decided to give. He asked them to walk toward it in obedience so they could receive what was theirs by promise, not by conquest.

We should sit with how strange this must have felt on the ground. Jericho was one of the most fortified cities in the region, gates barred, walls thick, defenders armed and ready. From a purely military standpoint, the idea that this city now belonged to Israel was, on its face, absurd. Nothing about the visible circumstances supported the claim. And yet the claim was already true, because it did not depend on visible circumstances. It depended on the character and the word of God, who does not speak in maybes.

This is precisely the shape of the inheritance we have been given in Christ. We are told that we have been blessed with every spiritual blessing, that we have been made alive, that we have been seated with Him in the heavenly places. Notice the tense again. Not someday. Already. The decisive word over our lives has already been spoken, sealed at the cross and confirmed at the empty tomb, long before we feel its full weight in our daily experience. Our sanctification, our final glory, our place in the household of God, all of it rests on a completed declaration, not a pending negotiation.

This truth ought to reorder the way we approach our own struggles. So much of our spiritual anxiety comes from treating promises as though they are still contested, as though we must fight, manipulate, or perform our way into what God has already settled. We pray as though we are trying to convince a reluctant judge rather than walk in step with a Father who has already ruled in our favor. We strive for identity, security, and acceptance that were never ours to win in the first place, because they were given.

None of this erases the walking. Israel still had to march. They still had to obey specific, sometimes uncomfortable instructions delivered by God through Joshua. The gift being already decided did not make the obedience optional. It made the obedience the means of reception rather than the means of achievement. There is a real difference between working for a wage and walking toward a gift, and Israel's posture at Jericho was the second, not the first.

We are called to the same posture. The promises attached to our salvation, our purpose, and our future are not prizes we chase down through sheer effort or spiritual hustle. They are declarations already made by a God who does not change His mind and does not go back on His word. Our task is not to secure what has already been secured. Our task is to walk in the obedience that receives it, trusting that the God who said "I have delivered" is not a God who says such things lightly.

If we are waiting on something today, wondering whether God's promise over our life still stands, Jericho answers the question before the walls ever fall. The delivering was already finished. All that remained was the walking.
