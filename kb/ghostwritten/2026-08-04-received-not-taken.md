There is a way of reading the book of Joshua that turns it into a story about military conquest, a nation seizing territory by the strength of its own arms. That reading misses the point entirely. Joshua is not a book about a people who took a land. It is a book about a covenant people who received a land, and that reception happened to involve military engagement along the way. The difference between taking and receiving is not a matter of semantics. It is the difference between two entirely different postures toward God.

To take something is to seize it by force, on our own authority, according to our own timing. To receive something is to accept a gift from someone who already owns it and has the right to give it. When the children of Israel crossed the Jordan on dry ground and stood at last on the plains of Jericho, they were not embarking on a war of acquisition. They were stepping into an inheritance that had already been promised, sealed, and guaranteed centuries earlier.

We remember that God spoke to Abraham in Genesis twelve and told him that this land would belong to his descendants forever. God even told Abraham, in advance, that his people would be enslaved for four hundred years before the promise came to pass. That warning was not a hedge or a qualification. It was proof that the promise was never dependent on Israel's strength, timing, or cleverness. It rested entirely on the character of the God who made it. When Abraham fell into a deep sleep during the covenant ceremony of Genesis fifteen and watched God alone pass between the halves of the sacrificed animals, the message could not have been clearer. This covenant would be guaranteed by God, not by Abraham's performance.

That is the theological backdrop we need before we can rightly read Joshua chapter five. When the Amorite and Canaanite kings heard that the Lord, Yahweh Himself, had dried up the Jordan before Israel, their hearts melted in fear and their courage failed them. Notice carefully what verse one tells us they feared. They were not primarily afraid of Israel's army. They were afraid of Israel's God. Rahab had said the same thing back in chapter two: word had reached Canaan of what this God had done at the Red Sea, and now they were watching Him do it again, this time in their own backyard. In the ancient world, people generally believed that gods were regional, that a deity's authority stopped at a river or a border. For Israel's God to act with the same power in Canaan that He had shown in Egypt was theologically disorienting to everyone watching. Whatever this God was, He clearly was not confined to a territory. He was not one god among many local gods. He was sovereign over all of it.

This is precisely why the natural, human instinct in this chapter is so wrong, and so revealing. Every combat-minded reader wants Israel to strike while the enemy is afraid. The kings are terrified, the army has just crossed on dry ground, this is the moment to attack. And yet that is not what happens. Instead, God tells Joshua to circumcise the nation. Before a single sword is drawn against Jericho, Israel is called to worship, to covenant renewal, to remembrance. The fear that grips Canaan in verse one is never used by Israel as a tactical advantage. It belongs to God, and God alone decides what to do with it. This is the tell that unlocks the whole book. Israel does not take this land through cunning or aggression. God gives it, and Israel's task is to receive it in a posture of submission rather than a posture of conquest.

This distinction matters far beyond ancient military history. It matters because it is the same distinction we are called to make about everything God has promised us. The temptation in our own lives is always to convert promise into project, to take matters into our own hands and manufacture by force what God intended to give as a gift. We do this with provision, with relationships, with ministry opportunities, with the very blessings God has already ordained for us. We forget that receiving requires patience, humility, and trust in the character of the giver, while taking requires only our own effort and timing.

It also matters for how we defend the honesty and goodness of this text against its critics. Joshua is regularly accused of glorifying genocide, of celebrating a nation stealing land from its rightful inhabitants. But that accusation depends entirely on the taking framework. If God owns all things, and if God has the sovereign right to determine who receives what and when, then this is not theft. It is inheritance, distributed by the rightful owner of everything. We can disagree about how to feel regarding the judgment enacted on Canaan's inhabitants, and Scripture does not ask us to be silent on that discomfort. But we cannot rightly call this conquest without first reckoning with the claim the text itself makes: this land belongs to God, and He is giving it to whom He chooses.

The promised land, then, was never Israel's to take. It was always God's to give. And what was true for Israel at the Jordan remains true for us today. Every good gift we receive, every promise fulfilled in our lives, comes not because we seized it but because a sovereign God, who owns everything, chose in His timing to give it. Our posture, like Israel's at the water's edge, is not to strike while we have the advantage. It is to submit, to trust, and to receive.
