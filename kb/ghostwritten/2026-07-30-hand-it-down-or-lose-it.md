The twelve stones at Gilgal were not built to be self-explanatory. A pile of rocks beside the road does not come with an inscription. It does not tell its own story. Left alone, it is just weight and silence, a landmark without language. God knew this when He commanded Joshua to build it, and He built the silence into the design on purpose. The memorial was engineered to raise a question. "In the future, when your children ask their parents, what do these stones mean?" That question was the whole point. The stones existed to prompt it, and the parents of Israel were responsible to answer it. Which means the Gilgal memorial only functioned if the generation who crossed the Jordan was ready to speak when the moment came.

This is a more demanding assignment than it might appear at first. It is one thing to experience a miracle. It is another thing entirely to understand it well enough to explain it to someone who was not there, someone who has no memory of the river, no frame of reference for what it meant to stand on dry ground where water had been running hours before, no category yet for the God who made it happen. The parents who would one day face that question from their children needed more than a memory. They needed a theology. They needed to know not just what happened but why it mattered, not just that the Jordan stopped but what it revealed about the God who stopped it and the covenant He had been keeping since Genesis.

Joshua gives them the language in verse twenty-two through twenty-four. Tell them Israel crossed the Jordan on dry ground. Tell them the Lord your God dried up the Jordan before you as He had dried up the Red Sea. Tell them He did this so all the peoples of the earth might know that the hand of the Lord is powerful, and so that you might always fear the Lord your God. The answer to the children's question is not simply a recitation of events. It is a theological declaration about the character and power of the covenant God, offered in the context of a story that began before anyone then living could remember and would continue past any horizon they could see.

This is what intergenerational faith transmission actually requires. It is not enough to bring children to a building on a regular basis and hope the institution does the work. The Gilgal model places the weight of transmission squarely on the parents. The stones prompt the question. The parents answer it. The building, the monument, the visible sign, is only the occasion for a conversation that has to happen in the home, around the table, in the car, on the ordinary days when no one is performing and the faith either shows up or it doesn't. Joshua 4 is not a passage about religious programming. It is a passage about the responsibility of those who have seen God work to tell the people who haven't.

The Hebrew word translated as children in verse twenty-one is the same word that can mean children, sons, grandsons, or descendants across many generations. The language does not make the generational distinction we make in English. Ben covers all of it. Which means the question Joshua anticipates is not just the question of the next generation but of every generation that comes after, standing in front of a pile of stones that is older than they are, wanting to know what it means and why it is there. The answer has to keep being given. The story has to keep being told. The memorial depends entirely on the willingness of each generation to receive the story and then hand it forward.

There is a weight in this that we can miss if we treat faith as primarily a personal matter. What God has done in our lives is not only ours to hold privately. It is covenant inheritance we are responsible to pass on. The things He has done in our marriages, in our seasons of crisis, in the moments where we had no human resources left and He showed up anyway, those stories belong to the people who come after us. When we keep them to ourselves, whether out of privacy or humility or simply because we never stopped to think about it, we are withholding something that was never meant to stay with us alone.

And the weight we carry in how we live matters as much as the stories we tell. The twelve men who pulled stones from the Jordan were carrying them on their shoulders all the way to Gilgal. There was nothing convenient about it. The stones were heavy and the road was long. Our children and grandchildren and the younger people coming up behind us in the church see the weight we carry before they hear the stories we tell. They see how we respond when the unexpected hits. They see whether we pray or panic, whether we hold or fold, whether we point to God or to our own ingenuity when something goes right. The memorial is not only what we say when the question comes. It is how we live in the days when no one is asking.

The generation standing at Gilgal had walked through two miracles. They had felt the dry ground of the Red Sea under their feet as children and the dry ground of the Jordan under their feet as adults. They had more than enough story to answer the question. The question is whether we have been paying attention to our own story, whether we have been watching what God has done closely enough that we can speak about it clearly when the moment comes. The pile of stones raises the question. The answer is ours to give. Every generation of the covenant people has had to decide whether they would give it faithfully or let the stones go silent.

We are this generation. The stones are in front of us. The question is coming.
