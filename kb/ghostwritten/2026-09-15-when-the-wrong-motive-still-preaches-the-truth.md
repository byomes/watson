Few things are more likely to steal our peace than discovering that someone doing good work is doing it for bad reasons. We feel it instinctively, that sense of offense when a person accomplishes something genuinely valuable while pursuing it for entirely selfish ends. It bothers us in the workplace, it bothers us in politics, and it bothers us intensely in the church, where we often assume that ministry should only ever flow from pure motives or not flow at all. The Apostle Paul addressed this exact tension while writing from a Roman prison cell, and his response to it should reshape how we handle it in our own lives.

Paul knew there were people in Rome actively preaching Christ while he sat in custody, and he knew their motives were not admirable. Some of them, he wrote, preached out of envy and rivalry. Worse still, he understood that certain preachers were doing this specifically to make his imprisonment harder, hoping that stirring up controversy in the streets would somehow increase his suffering behind bars. This was not simply competitive ambition. It was a deliberate attempt to use the gospel as a weapon against a man they viewed as a rival. Any reasonable person would expect Paul to be furious about this, and by his own admission it would have been an understandable response.

Instead, Paul arrived at a conclusion that should startle us every time we read it. What does it matter, he asked, whether the motive is false or true. The important thing is that in every way, Christ is being preached, and because of this he rejoiced and would continue to rejoice. He did not defend the wrong motives. He did not pretend they were acceptable or excuse the selfish ambition behind them. He simply refused to let the impurity of the messenger overshadow the truth of the message. Christ was being proclaimed, and that fact mattered more to Paul than his personal grievance against the people proclaiming Him.

This distinction carries real weight for how we engage with a world that rarely delivers truth through spotless vessels. We are surrounded by imperfect people saying true things, and we are equally surrounded by imperfect people saying false things, and our task is not to sort every message primarily by the character of its messenger. If we require perfect motives before we will acknowledge that something true has been spoken, we will spend our entire lives disqualifying truth on the basis of impurity we find in the person who spoke it. That standard, if applied consistently, would disqualify every single one of us, since none of our own motives are ever entirely pure either.

This does not mean motive is irrelevant or that we should stop caring about the integrity of those who teach and lead within the church. Paul cared about motive deeply, which is precisely why he named the difference between goodwill and selfish ambition so directly. Those who acted out of rivalry were still accountable before God for the condition of their hearts, and nothing in this passage suggests otherwise. What Paul refused to do was let his legitimate concern over motive become the lens through which he evaluated whether the gospel itself had value in that moment. He could hold both truths simultaneously. Wrong motives are genuinely wrong, and Christ being preached is genuinely good, and the second reality does not cancel or excuse the first.

We would do well to learn this same posture in our own lives, particularly in an age saturated with public discourse where motive is endlessly scrutinized and often used as a weapon to dismiss whatever a person has said. It has become common practice to disregard an argument entirely once we have decided the person making it has questionable intentions. Paul offers us a more disciplined alternative. Evaluate the truth on its own terms. Hold the messenger accountable separately, according to what they deserve, without allowing that accountability to distort whether the actual content of what was said carries weight.

There is a kind of freedom in this posture that we rarely access. Paul was not naive about the people preaching against him in bad faith, and he was certainly not required to trust them or befriend them. But he refused to let their impurity rob him of joy over the one thing that mattered most, which was that people were hearing about Christ. If we can learn that same discipline, separating our legitimate concern over human motive from our confidence in the truth itself, we will find ourselves far less rattled by a world that rarely gives us the luxury of pure messengers, and far more anchored to the truth those imperfect messengers sometimes manage to speak.
