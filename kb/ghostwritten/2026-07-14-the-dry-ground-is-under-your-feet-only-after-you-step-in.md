We tend to prefer a faith that confirms itself before it costs us anything. We want to see the water part before we step into the river. We want the road to become clear before we leave the driveway. We want enough evidence in advance to make the decision feel less like a leap and more like a reasonable next step. And God, in His patience and generosity, sometimes works that way. But the Jordan crossing in Joshua chapter three is not that kind of story.

The Jordan River was at flood stage. The text is deliberate about this detail — not a gentle stream, not a manageable ford, but a deep, fast-moving, dangerous crossing at the worst possible time of year. Joshua's instructions to the priests were simple: take up the Ark of the Covenant and walk to the water's edge. And when you reach the water's edge, go and stand in the river. Not wait beside it. Not pray over it. Step into it.

What makes this remarkable is the sequence. God did not stop the water and then ask the priests to cross. He asked the priests to cross and then stopped the water. Verse fifteen marks the moment precisely: as soon as the priests who carried the Ark reached the Jordan and their feet touched the water's edge, the water from upstream stopped flowing. The miracle followed the obedience. It did not precede it.

This is not a small theological detail. It is the structure of faith as Scripture consistently presents it. Abraham left Ur without a map. Noah built an ark before a drop of rain had fallen. The disciples left their nets before they understood where Jesus was taking them. In each case, the movement came first and the confirmation came after. Not because God is indifferent to our need for assurance, but because faith, by its very nature, is trust extended in the direction of what is not yet visible. If we could see it clearly in advance, we would not need faith. We would just need information.

The priests carrying the Ark that morning had every human reason to hesitate. They were carrying the most sacred object in Israel — the visible symbol of God's covenant presence among His people. The prospect of stumbling in a flood-stage river, of losing footing on slippery riverbed stones, of dropping what they carried — these were not irrational concerns. They were the concerns of people who took their responsibility seriously. And yet the text records no hesitation. They walked to the water's edge. They stepped in. And the river stopped.

It is worth asking what would have happened if they had stopped at the bank. If they had stood at the water's edge and waited for a sign that it was safe — waited for the water to slow, or for a dry path to appear, or for some prior confirmation that God was going to do what Joshua said He would do. The answer the text implies is uncomfortable: Israel would not have seen the miracle. Because the miracle was contingent on the step. The sequence was not incidental. It was the point.

God was not withholding the miracle out of stubbornness. He was positioning His people in a posture of trust that the inheritance ahead of them would require. The promised land was not going to be won by people who waited for certainty before they moved. It was going to be inhabited by people who had learned to step into flood-stage rivers because God said to, and who had discovered, in doing so, that the ground became dry under their feet in the very act of stepping. That kind of confidence — not arrogance, but the settled assurance of people who have crossed rivers — is what the battles ahead would demand.

We carry this same invitation in our own moment. There are rivers in each of our lives — decisions, callings, crossings that God is asking us to make toward the inheritance He has prepared. And the pattern of Scripture is consistent: the dry ground appears under our feet only after we step in. Not before. The confirmation comes in the obedience, not instead of it. This does not mean recklessness — Joshua's step was in response to a specific word from God, not a leap into nothing. But it does mean that waiting for the river to part before we are willing to move is, in most cases, not prudence. It is the wilderness dressed up as wisdom.

The priests stepped in. The river stopped. Twenty-five miles of riverbed went dry. An entire nation crossed on solid ground. None of that would have happened if someone had decided the flood stage was too dangerous and chosen to wait on the bank for better conditions. The conditions became good in the stepping. They always do.
